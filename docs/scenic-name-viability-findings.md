**Status: screened 2026-09-20 against `main` at `3c35b4f`. Nothing was filed,
bought, reserved or renamed. Thirteen variations were generated and screened to
the depth of [`trademark-knockout-findings.md`](trademark-knockout-findings.md)
§12–§15, plus the bare word. The bare word is blocked on both bars. Every
variation that still reads as "scenic" is blocked. The four that survive the
screen survive by no longer meaning anything — which is the answer to the
question that was actually asked.**

Answers [`scenic-name-viability-brief.md`](scenic-name-viability-brief.md).
Extends the eight-name screen in
[`trademark-knockout-findings.md`](trademark-knockout-findings.md) with thirteen
more candidates and re-runs its positive control, which still passes. Three
corrections to inherited facts, each flagged where it is made: the

taken the definite article" is **true of the App Store and false of the

because **TSDR's JSON API has begun requiring an API key** (§17).

**Nobody here is a lawyer and nothing below is legal advice.** This is a

verdicts it is entitled to reach are set out in `trademark-knockout-findings.md`
under "This is a knockout search, not a clearance search". The words *clear* and

### What the examining attorney actually refused on

Retrieved from the office action itself (38 pages, TSDR document
`NFIN20260716205313`). The **only substantive ground maintained** is the one
that matters most to this question:

> **SUMMARY OF ISSUES that applicant must address:**
> * Section 2(e)(1) Refusal - Merely Descriptive
> * Identification of Goods and Services Requirement

There is **no §2(d) likelihood-of-confusion refusal.** The application is not
failing because somebody else owns SCENIC. It is failing because the USPTO does
not think anybody can own it in this field. Two requirements — email address and
US-licensed counsel — were satisfied and dropped.

The examining attorney's reasoning, from the action:

- The Merriam-Webster and American Heritage evidence shows SCENIC means *"of or
  relating to natural scenery"* and *"constituting or affording pleasing views
  of natural features"* — so applied to travel and tour services it
  *"immediately and directly conveys to consumers some information about
  them."*
- Applicant argued it is *"improbable"* for one term to be descriptive across
  nine international classes. Rejected: a mark may be merely descriptive **even
  if it does not describe the full scope and extent** of the goods, and
  describing **one** significant attribute is enough.
- Applicant argued five years of use. Rejected: **no §2(f) acquired-
  distinctiveness claim was made**, and allegations of sales and advertising do
  not by themselves establish acquired distinctiveness.
- And the paragraph that decides this question for our purposes:

> Applicant has provided a list of registrations involving the term SCENIC which
> belong to applicant's sister company … it is noted that these registrations
> show that SCENIC has been **repeatedly disclaimed** for services in Classes
> 35, 39, and 43.

**The applicant's own corporate family has already disclaimed the word.** That
is not this document's characterisation — it is verifiable on the register, and
§4 verifies it independently.

### What this does to the argument against "Scenic"

It **changes its shape and strengthens it.** The brief's §2 framed the register
risk as *"if it registers, the exact word becomes a live US registration in this
field."* That framing now looks like the wrong worry:

- If it **registers**, it will only have done so by overcoming a descriptiveness
  refusal — most likely via §2(f), which is an admission the word is descriptive
  and yields a mark narrow enough to leave ordinary descriptive use alone.
- If it **goes abandoned**, the brief predicted the register argument against
  "Scenic" disappears. **It does not.** What is left behind is a written finding
  by a USPTO examining attorney, twice, that SCENIC is merely descriptive of
  travel software and services — in class 9 for downloadable travel apps and
  class 42 for travel-planning SaaS, which are this product's exact classes.

**Either way the register now says the same thing: this word is not ownable
here.** That cuts against a competitor asserting it — and equally against this
project ever owning it.

the failure mode that caught
*Aimless* in `trademark-knockout-findings.md` §4 — a store check rated it clean
one day before a competitor launched. **A "scenic" store count has a short shelf
life, and it is trending the wrong way.**

## 13. The bare word: verdict

**Blocked, drop it.** It fails both bars independently, and the failures are of
different kinds so neither rescues the other.

**Legal bar.** The word is merely descriptive of software that finds scenic
routes. That is not this document's opinion — a USPTO examining attorney has now
written it twice, in 2025 and again in 2026, refusing it in classes 9 and 42 for
travel software with dictionary evidence and thirty-plus third-party
registrations where the word is disclaimed. Eighty-two marks disclaim it, 43 of
them in this product's classes, including three held by the applicant's own
corporate family. **The upside case for this name — that it becomes an ownable
asset — is closed by a primary source.**

**App Store bar.** A senior, identical, directly-competing Navigation app under
the exact word, 4.7★ across 7,435 ratings, released 2016, updated three weeks
ago, with a paid tier to protect — and a nine-app `Scenic Map` family in the
same category with priority to 2010. At least 29 Navigation/Travel apps carry
the word and eleven of them arrived this year. Guideline 5.2.1 asks whether a
name is a copycat representation; a new Navigation app called *Scenic* is the
easiest version of that complaint anyone will ever have to write.

**And the two interact badly.** Because the word is descriptive, the app could
never have built a defence out of owning it; because a senior user is
established, the app carries the risk of using it anyway. **All of the exposure,
none of the asset.**

## 14. The variation that is not a name

The brief asked for variations the owner might reasonably propose next. The most
useful one is not a mark at all: **keep "scenic" in the subtitle, keywords and
description, and put a different word on the app.**

This is a different question with a different answer, and the answer is
favourable. Apple's guideline **2.3.7**, verbatim:

> Choose a unique app name, assign keywords that accurately describe your app,
> and don't try to pack any of your metadata with trademarked terms, popular app
> names, pricing information, or other irrelevant phrases just to game the
> system. … App subtitles … should not include inappropriate content, reference
> other apps, or make unverifiable product claims.

What 2.3.7 prohibits is **packing metadata to game the system** and **referencing
other apps**. Using "scenic" as ordinary descriptive English — *finds the scenic
route*, *scores every road for scenery* — is the opposite of both: it is a
keyword that accurately describes the app, which is what the guideline asks for
in its first clause. The word is descriptive, which is precisely why it cannot
be owned by the competitor either.

**Two things to keep straight, and they are the failure modes:**

1. **Field by field.** 2.3.7 governs name, subtitle and keywords separately —
   the guideline text caps the *name* at 30 characters and then addresses
   subtitles in their own sentence. [`victory-lap-naming.md`](victory-lap-naming.md)
   §6 holds this project's measured field limits. A word that is a problem in
   the name is not automatically a problem in the description.
2. **Descriptive use, not naming use.** *"Scenic routes, scored"* is descriptive.
   *"The scenic app"* is a name wearing a subtitle's clothes, and hands over the
   same complaint. The line is whether the word is doing a job an adjective
   does.

This is Trap 4 of the brief restated as a product decision rather than a
codebase rule: **the vocabulary stays, the mark goes.** The correct-English uses
of "scenic" in the code — *scenic route*, *scenic score*, *scenic km*, *scenic
arm*, *scenic byway* — and the **1,966** uses in `docs/route-census/`, where it
is a data column value, were not touched by this screen and should not be.

## 15. What the candidate set actually shows

The brief offered a structural hypothesis and asked for it to be tested rather
than adopted. **It is confirmed, and it has a consequence the brief did not
anticipate.**

Confirmed: the descriptive compounds are worse than the bare word, not better.
*Scenic Route*, *Scenic Drive*, *Scenic Way*, *Scenics*, *Scenery* are each
blocked by something the bare word is not blocked by — a live registration in
class 9 or 39, or a live app of the same name in Navigation — **in addition to
carrying the same descriptiveness problem.** Adding a noun to a descriptive
adjective produces a phrase that names the goods exactly, and phrases that name
the goods exactly are what everybody else in the category reached for too.

The consequence: **the survivors survive by ceasing to be the word.**

`Scenix`, `Scenica`, `Skenic` and `Skene` pass because a coined string cannot be
descriptive of anything. But that is the same property that makes them fail the
purpose of asking. The reason the owner wants "Scenic" back is that it is *the
accurate word for what the product does* — and none of the four survivors
communicates that to anyone. A user seeing *Skenic* does not think of scenery;
they think it is a typo. *Scenix* reads as a 1990s semiconductor. *Scenica*
reads as a furniture line. *Skene* reads as a surname, or worse.

**So the answer to "is any variation of Scenic viable" has two halves and they
point in the same direction:**

- **Every variation that still reads as "scenic" is blocked** — nine of
  thirteen, and the tenth (*Scenic Miles*) walks into the §2(e)(1) refusal that
  §3 documents.
- **Every variation that is not blocked no longer reads as "scenic"** — so it
  delivers none of the thing that made the question worth asking, while costing
  exactly as much to adopt as any unrelated word would.

There is no variation in this set that is both recognisably "Scenic" and
unobstructed, and §7's evidence suggests there is no such thing to find: the
obstruction and the recognisability have the same cause. The word is a good
description, so everyone uses it, so nobody can have it.

**This is not a recommendation.** Four candidates reached "no knockout blocker
found" and any of them could be taken to clearance. The choice is the owner's.
