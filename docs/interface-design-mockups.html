Sunday Drive — the interface, drawn

Companion to docs/interface-design.md, written against main at d11344d. Nothing here is built. Every screen is 393×852 pt, portrait iPhone. The app is dark by default, so dark leads each pair; light is fully designed and is one switch away in Sources. Apple’s map attribution is drawn as a neutral grey placeholder rather than reproducing Apple’s mark; the reserved space is the point. Numbers are plausible for the trips named and consistent across screens.

1 · Cold launch

Directions or a loop, on the first screen

The loop is one tap from a cold start — it already has a place (here) and a length (whatever you asked for last time). Today it is behind a segmented control, a start field and a distance slider. The map is a card, so Apple’s attribution sits inside it, bottom-left, with nothing over it at any scroll position. The credit line is pinned under the page and never scrolls. newRecent destinations — five entries in UserDefaults, the mechanism the voice picker already uses.

2 · Setting the ends

The map shrinks while you type

The keyboard takes the bottom half of the screen. A page gives it that space by scrolling and shortening the map; a sheet has to fight it — which is the origin of the onChange(of: focused) nudge at RoutePanel.swift:238, the stale hit-test frame, and the panel that appeared to close itself. Swap and clear sit with the fields they act on. The credit line survives at the only place left: above the keyboard, still without interaction.

3 · The trade

What the setting costs and what it buys

The main planning screen. The dial’s readout replaces scenery strength 0.25 (RoutePanel.swift:484) with what that setting costs and what it buys. The ledger’s small slate figures are the fastest route — a reference price, not a second product: you cannot start it, and the dial’s left end already is it, exactly. The handle’s position is still the router’s strength under PrefSlider’s measured sqrt mapping; only the caption changed. Bars carry the six scenery hues, which are also the legend for the line on the map.

4 · The trade, mid-drag

No figures until the route is recomputed

While the finger is down the route has not been recomputed, so the readout shows the name of the region of the track instead of a figure that would be wrong for half a second. The ledger and the bars dim to say so. On release the request goes, and the numbers roll in with contentTransition(.numericText()). Right: the degenerate case at pref == 0, where server/app.py short-circuits and the two arms are one route — the ledger is replaced rather than annotated.

5 · Loop

The loop, set in time rather than distance

The request is a distance; what a driver has is a span of time. It still goes to /api/loop in kilometres, and the label converts — using the km-per-minute ratio of the last loop the server returned, so the estimate calibrates itself with no backend change and is replaced by the truth the moment the loop lands. repeated_km stays always-visible, because it is the one number that tells a driver their loop is really an out-and-back — it just stops being orange, which in this palette means an alert.

6 · What you like

The scenery weights, and a chip that shows them

The entry point is a chip reading Coast & water, not a button labelled Tune: the current screen can tell you something is set but not what. Each slider carries its type’s colour, so this sheet, the breakdown bars and the map speak one language. The Town centers note stays exactly as written — a product admitting a measurement went against it is worth keeping, and it sets the register for everything else.

7 · Compliance, as designed elements

The credit line, and the safety notice

Left: Sources. Every credit string is unchanged — they are somebody else’s text, checked against the licensors and reproduced verbatim. What changes is that the sentence explaining why you are reading this becomes the largest thing on the screen, and the six scenery hues appear against the datasets that produce them. Right: Before you drive, shown once on first launch and reachable for ever after from Sources — one view, one copy of the fixed notice, two ways in. The obligation is the natural host for the app’s honest limits, which is better than burying both.

8 · The drive

Five things, floating clear of the bottom-left

The banner is opaque, not .ultraThinMaterial. The codebase already learned this one screen down — the verdict buttons were moved to .regularMaterial because glass over a map is a tint, not a background, and POI labels read straight through it (NavView.swift:387). The maneuver is the one thing read at 50 mph in sunlight; it gets ink. Everything floats with a margin, and the bottom 48 pt above the safe-area inset is empty — on a heading-up map that is the road already driven, the least valuable pixels on the display.

9 · The score ribbon needs one API field

What is coming up

The app scores 236,000 km of road and, from behind the wheel, none of that is visible. A ten-point strip up the right edge shows the beauty of the next five miles, coloured by what makes it beautiful — so the driver can see the coast arriving. This needs score_profile: [Double] on the route response, one value per vertex of the LineString the API already returns; it is the same information as scenery_km, positioned. Without it the ribbon is simply absent and nothing else changes. It is the single highest-value thing the interface wants from the backend.

10 · The keep

The bottom-left of every map belongs to Apple

ADPLA Attachment 6 §2.1 requires the logo and attribution visible; §4 makes obscuring the logo grounds for revoking MapKit access. The current build covers it at every detent, because a .sheet is a separate presentation layer and the map beneath cannot lay its attribution out around it. Two applications of one rule, drawn below. On the right, for comparison, what ships today.
